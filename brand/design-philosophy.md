# Below the Waterline — a design philosophy

**The movement.** Below the Waterline is an aesthetic of hidden structure: the
conviction that the most important part of any vessel is the part no one sees.
It celebrates the quiet engineering that keeps things upright — ballast, spine,
keel — and renders it with the reverence of a naval draughtsman's plate. Every
composition is a cross-section: a thin horizon, a calm mass above, and beneath
it the dense, deliberate geometry that does the real work.

**Space and form.** The canvas divides at a single horizontal datum — the
waterline — placed with surgical care. Above it: vast, breathing emptiness.
Below it: concentrated form, drawn in unbroken strokes of constant weight,
rounded at every terminus like tooling polished by ten thousand passes. Forms
are few and exact: one basin curve, one vertical spine, one counterweight.
Nothing decorative survives; whatever remains must bear load.

**Color and material.** Two materials only, with one signal. Deep sounding-chart
navy — the color of water at forty fathoms — and warm chart-paper cream, each
serving as figure or ground depending on the light. A single drop of coral
marks the ballast point: the smallest element carries the warmest color,
because that is where the weight lives. The palette must feel calibrated, not
chosen — the product of painstaking chromatic testing, mixed and re-mixed until
the navy holds the cream without swallowing it.

**Scale and rhythm.** Stroke weight is a constant, like a class rule in naval
architecture: one gauge for every line, so the mark reads identically at the
size of a fingernail or the width of a wall. Rhythm comes from interval, not
repetition — the measured gap between waterline and hull, hull and spine, spine
and ballast. These intervals follow the proportions of a stable hull: wider
than tall above the datum, deeper than wide below it.

**Typography and hierarchy.** Words are cargo: stowed low, packed tight,
minimal. A single lowercase wordmark set in a round geometric sans, letterforms
echoing the basin curve. Auxiliary text appears only as engineer's marginalia —
small monospaced station labels, the kind found etched beside a frame number on
a builder's drawing. Text never explains; it registers. The hierarchy is
absolute: mark, then name, then whisper.

**Craft.** Every plate must look like it was drafted by a master loftsman at
the top of the trade — measured twice, faired by hand, inked once. Curves are
faired until no kink survives magnification; terminals align to a hidden grid;
optical corrections are made where mathematics alone would look wrong. The
finished artifact should feel meticulously crafted, labored over with care —
an object that proves stability itself can be drawn.
